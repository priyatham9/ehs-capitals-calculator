Each bar spans the payback you get when that one input is moved to each end of its range while every other input is held at its current value. The vertical line is the current answer. One-at-a-time analysis ignores inputs moving together, so it understates joint risk.

Risk reduction is an input, not an output. This page does not know how effective your control will be. It takes the number you type and does arithmetic with it. The tornado and breakeven views exist to show you how much of the answer rests on that one assumption.
Method, and what this does not do
  • The arithmetic. annual saving = annual incident cost × risk reduction; payback = capex ÷ annual saving; ROI = (annual saving × horizon) ÷ capex. That is the whole model.
  • ROI here is cumulative benefit over investment, not net return. A 147% ROI means benefits reached 1.47× the capital cost, not that you cleared 147% profit. Net ROI is shown alongside it and is always exactly 100 points lower.
  • Nothing is discounted. A dollar saved in year 5 counts the same as one saved in year 1. At any realistic cost of capital this flatters long horizons, and the effect grows with the horizon you choose.
  • Benefits are assumed flat and permanent. One risk reduction, one annual cost, unchanged for the whole horizon. No degradation, no maintenance cost, no residual value.
  • The human-capital lines are estimates. Lost work time, retraining, productivity and morale are real costs, but they are modelled, not invoiced. Their size is a judgement, and the second accounting inherits every bit of that uncertainty.
  • Counting more cost can never weaken the case. Because every added line is non-negative, the human-capital accounting always shows payback at least as fast. That is a property of the model's structure, not evidence found in data. Treat it as a reason for scepticism, not confirmation.
