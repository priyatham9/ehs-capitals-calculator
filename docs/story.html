The capital case - story 5 of 6

The same control, counted twice, gets two answers

A $150,000 loading-station control pays back in 3.41 years on the booked ledger and 1.59 years once the human-capital lines are priced. The control did not change. The accounting did.

3.41Booked payback, years 1.59Human-capital payback, years

Step 01

What the ledger shows

Downtime, spilled material, injury costs. Three lines, all of them invoiced, all of them already in a system somebody owns. They total $55,000 a year at this station.

Annual incident cost counted $55,000

Step 02

The arithmetic on those three lines

Assume the control removes 80% of that cost. The annual saving is $44,000. Divide the $150,000 capital cost by it and the payback lands at 3.41 years, past a 3-year hurdle.

Payback 3.41 yr · 5-year ROI 147%

Fails the hurdle
Step 03

The costs nobody invoices

Lost work time, retraining after turnover, productivity loss, the morale hit on the crew. Four more lines, $63,000 a year, real but unbooked. They belong to the same incidents.

Annual incident cost counted $118,000

Step 04

Same control, different verdict

The saving becomes $94,400 and the payback drops to 1.59 years. Nothing about the equipment moved. The scope of the count moved.

Payback 1.59 yr · 5-year ROI 315%

Clears the hurdle

What the answer rests on

Move each of the nine inputs on its own. Two of them carry the whole case, and neither one is invoiced.

What the answer rests on

Step 05

Nine inputs, one at a time

Risk reduction, capital cost, and each of the seven cost lines. Each one is pushed 30% up and 30% down while the other eight hold at their base value.

Step 06

Two bars carry the case

Risk reduction swings the payback by a full year. Capital cost swings it by 0.95. The widest cost line, equipment downtime, moves it 0.24.

Risk reduction 1.27 to 2.27 yr · capital cost 1.11 to 2.07 yr

Step 07

The widest bar is the one you invented

Risk reduction is not measured here. It is typed in. The tool multiplies by whatever number you supply, and that number moves the answer more than every cost line put together.

Two thousand versions

Run the same decision 2,000 times with every input jittered. The spread is wide. It is not what decides this.

Scene three

Two thousand versions of the same decision

Draw every input independently across the same ±30% window, 2,000 times, seeded so the run reproduces. Each draw goes through the same four-line model and returns one payback.

Step 08

Let it run

Cost lines, capital cost, and risk reduction each get their own draw inside the ±30% band. Two thousand paybacks land in the bins.

Step 09

Where the human-capital case lands

The median draw pays back in 1.60 years, the 5th percentile in 1.05, the 95th in 2.43. Almost every draw clears the hurdle.

P(payback within 3 yr) 99.9%

Step 10

The booked ledger, through the same machine

Same seed, same spread, same model, three cost lines instead of seven. Now the median draw pays back in 3.40 years and fewer than a third of the draws clear the hurdle.

P(payback within 3 yr) 31.3%

Step 11

Uncertainty did not decide this

Put both runs on the same axis. The middle 90% of the human-capital draws lands between 1.05 and 2.43 years; the booked draws land between 2.22 and 5.45. The gap between them is the scope of the count, not the noise in the inputs.

Overlap 2.22 to 2.43 yr

What this does not establish

  • No empirical data is used anywhere in this repository. There is no dataset and no fitted parameter. The worked example is transcribed from one published article's cost table; everything else is arithmetic on numbers a user supplies.
  • Counting more cost can never weaken the case, by construction. The human-capital accounting is a strict superset of the booked one, so adding lines always shortens the payback. That is a property of the arithmetic, not a finding about controls.
  • Risk reduction is an input, not a measurement. The tool does not know your control or your failure modes. It multiplies by whatever you type, and the tornado shows most of the conclusion resting on that one number.
  • The human-capital lines are estimates, not invoices. Lost work time, retraining, productivity, morale. Each is a priced judgement, and it should be labelled as one when it reaches a finance reviewer.
  • Nothing is discounted and benefits are assumed flat. A dollar saved in year five counts the same as one saved today, and the control is assumed to keep working at the same rate for the whole horizon.
  • ROI here is cumulative benefit over investment, not net return. 147% means benefits reached 1.47 times the capital cost. Net ROI is always exactly 100 points lower: 47% and 215%.
  • Monte Carlo propagates uncertainty, it does not reduce it. The ±30% window is a choice. Draws are independent, and in a real plant the cost lines move together.

Full list: README.md, "Limitations".

Monday

Take both paybacks to the committee

  1. Pull the next capital request that was rejected on payback. Write down the cost lines it counted.
  2. Price the four human-capital lines for the same incidents: lost work time, retraining after turnover, productivity loss, morale. Mark each one as an estimate and say who estimated it.
  3. Run both accountings, and state the risk reduction you assumed as an assumption, in the same sentence as the payback.
  4. Show the committee the tornado, not only the headline. If the case turns on a number you typed, they should see that before they vote.
